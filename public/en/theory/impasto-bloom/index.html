II. Colour and Painterly Matter

Impasto Bloom: Painterly Matter

Layered impasto painting

The method of layered impasto painting: the stroke as an independent structural element, painting into a partly dried layer, and the relation of flower and background.

Nona Dronova · Essay on method

Original source: the artist's digital archive HYDRANGEA-BLOOM, Peony-Expansion

Impasto Bloom is a philosophy in which the materiality of paint becomes a living organism.

The expressive power of these paintings is created through a method of layered brushwork. Rather than blending colours on the palette, each brushstroke is applied as an independent structural element that remains visible on the canvas.

The partly dried layer

An essential part of this process is allowing the previous paint layers to partially dry before new strokes are added. This creates a complex surface in which every layer preserves its own colour, texture and direction while interacting with those beneath it. Colours interact optically instead of blending into uniform tones.

Broad stroke and fine rhythm

The expressive character of a peony depends not only on colour but on the structure of the brushwork. Broad, confident strokes establish the flower's volume, while smaller overlapping strokes refine its rhythm and reveal the complexity of the petals. Each layer should remain visible, preserving the energy and direction of the artist's hand rather than disappearing into a smooth surface.

Equally important is the relationship between the peony and its background. A carefully considered contrast of value, temperature and saturation allows the flower to emerge naturally from the surrounding space. The background should not compete with the blossom but provide a dynamic visual counterpoint.

The bold superimposition of brushstrokes generates depth, rhythm and movement. Instead of producing a smooth illusion, the painting develops as a living structure, where light is reflected from countless textured surfaces.

Questions for looking

  1. Compare the detail with the whole painting: at what distance do the strokes ‘assemble’ into a flower?
  2. Where in the detail can the lower layer be seen through the upper one?

The Courage of the Contrasting Stroke

Brushstroke, rhythm and the living surface

Artist's text Text by Nona Dronova · Original English text by the artist. · Source: The-Theory-of-Pictorial-Contrasts

If colour establishes the energy of the painting, the brushstroke is the physical act through which that energy becomes visible.

The brushstroke is not simply a technical instrument. It carries colour, rhythm, movement and the physical presence of the painter. In expressionist botanical painting the stroke does not have to imitate the smoothness of nature. It can remain visible. It can be unexpected. It can contradict the colour surrounding it.

Not softening the contrast

A leaf may appear green, but it does not have to be constructed from green alone. A strong blue stroke can enter a green leaf; violet can appear beside yellow; turquoise can interrupt a warm surface. The instinct of the painter is often to soften such contrasts — to blend them until they become more comfortable. Dronova deliberately resists this impulse. The contrasting stroke must sometimes remain uncompromised. Its strength gives the surface its energy.

Freedom within structure

The painting develops through the accumulation of individual gestures. One stroke is placed beside another; a new colour partially covers an earlier layer; another stroke interrupts it. This process can appear spontaneous, even chaotic. But the apparent freedom is controlled by the overall structure of the painting. Its direction can reinforce the movement of a leaf. Its length can echo the rhythm of stems or petals. Its density can create depth. Its colour can connect one area of the canvas with another. The brushstroke operates on two levels: as an independent gesture and as part of the larger composition.

The aim is therefore not uncontrolled spontaneity, but disciplined freedom.

The living surface

The final surface should retain the memory of its creation. The viewer should sense that the painting was built through successive decisions, layers, contrasts and gestures. For Dronova, this visible process is part of the meaning of the work. The brushstroke is therefore not merely the way the painting is made. It is the way the painting speaks.

Questions for looking

  1. Find a stroke in the detail that ‘argues’ with the surrounding colour. What would change if it were softened?
  2. How does the direction of the strokes relate to the movement of petals or leaves?

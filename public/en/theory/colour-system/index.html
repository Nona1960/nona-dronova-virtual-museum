II. Colour and Painterly Matter

The Colour System: White, Blue, Red

Hydrangeas and foliage

Why white, blue and red hydrangeas call for different colour principles, and how reflected colour turns the flower head into a luminous, spatial form. Freedom in the stroke, discipline in colour.

Nona Dronova · Colour study

Original source: the artist's digital archive HYDRANGEA-BLOOM, Leaves-in-Painting

White, blue and red hydrangeas cannot be painted according to the same colour principles, as each possesses its own optical character, atmosphere and visual energy.

A convincing pictorial image emerges when local colour is enriched by contrasting reflections. It is these subtle colour interactions that transform the rounded flower head into a vibrant, luminous and spatial form. Contrast does not destroy harmony — it reveals the hidden energy of colour.

White hydrangeas

White is the most demanding colour in painting, because it does not exist as a flat surface. A convincing white hydrangea head is built from cool greys, pale greens and warm ivory notes borrowed from the surrounding light. Without these reflected undertones the flower flattens; with them, it gains volume, air and the sense of catching real daylight.

Blue hydrangeas

Blue hydrangeas carry the widest range of reflections — violet, teal, lavender and steel-grey shift within a single flower head. Set against the dark green of foliage, these cool tones intensify into something closer to stained glass. A few warm accents, gold or coral, are needed to keep the blue from settling into monotony.

Red and pink hydrangeas

Red and pink hydrangeas are built through the interplay of high-key rose and deeper crimson shadow. Their warmth is only legible against cool green foliage, which pushes the colour forward and gives it glow rather than flatness. Where blue asks for restraint, red and pink ask for boldness.

Freedom in the stroke, discipline in colour

“When painting white hydrangeas, I do not think of white as a single colour… the brushwork can remain spontaneous and seemingly chaotic. Painting leaves requires a different kind of discipline. The green of foliage is deceptively complex. One must not soften everything into a comfortable range of similar greens. The painter must see differences clearly — and have the courage to state them.”

Freedom in the brushstroke. Discipline in colour.

Questions for looking

  1. Which colours is the ‘white’ flower head actually built from?
  2. Find the warm accent that keeps the blue hydrangea from monotony.

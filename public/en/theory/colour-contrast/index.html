Colour as a Structural Force

The Theory of Pictorial Contrasts

Artist's text Text by Nona Dronova · Original English text by the artist. · Source: The-Theory-of-Pictorial-Contrasts

For Nona Dronova, colour is not an ornament added to form. It is one of the fundamental forces that constructs the painting.

In botanical painting nature often appears deceptively harmonious. A garden may seem to be composed simply of greens, interrupted by the colours of flowers. Yet attentive observation reveals a much more complex system of relationships. Red poppies can intensify the blue of irises. Yellow daylilies can make pale blue lupines appear colder and more luminous. Red and blue hydrangeas can create a powerful tension within the same composition.

The dominant contrast

A painting should not contain a collection of equally strong colour oppositions. Dronova begins by identifying a dominant colour contrast — the principal relationship around which the composition is organised. Red and blue may become the central opposition; yellow and blue may provide a secondary contrast. Other colours then support, connect and balance these dominant forces. The purpose is not to make every colour equally powerful. It is to establish a hierarchy of colour.

Colour must travel through the painting

A contrasting colour should not remain confined to the object from which it originates. If a painting contains red poppies and blue irises, traces of red may enter the surrounding foliage, shadows or neighbouring forms. Blue may appear unexpectedly within the greens around the poppies. Through these repetitions, colours begin to travel across the canvas. A colour that first belongs to one flower becomes part of the entire pictorial space. This creates chromatic continuity.

Beyond complementary colours

Colour contrast is not limited to traditional complementary pairs. Dronova also works with relationships between warm and cool, light and dark, saturated and muted, transparent and dense, luminous and subdued. These contrasts can operate simultaneously, creating a complex chromatic structure. The objective is not simply intensity. It is tension, depth and visual energy.

Recognition and transformation

The flower must remain recognisable. But its colour does not have to remain entirely obedient to optical reality. A blue iris may contain violet, turquoise and unexpected warm notes. A green leaf may contain yellow, blue or purple. A red flower may become more intense than the natural colour observed in the garden. This is the point at which botanical observation becomes expressionist painting. The natural object provides the starting point. Colour transforms it into an artistic reality.

The painting is therefore not a collection of flowers. It is a system of interacting colour energies.

Questions for looking

  1. Find the dominant colour contrast in the work. Which contrast is secondary?
  2. Trace where the brightest colour ‘travels’: which neighbouring forms does it enter?
  3. Which pairs other than complementaries are at work: warm and cool, dense and transparent?
